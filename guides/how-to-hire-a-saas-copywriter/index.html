How to hire a SaaS copywriter

Hire the person who asks the best questions about your buyer. Words are cheap now. Knowing which claim your buyer will believe is the hard part, and it is what you pay for.

By Blake Emal · October 30, 2026

Four ways to get copy

Each one works well for a certain kind of team. None is right for everyone.

  • 1

    Hire a freelancer

    One independent writer you hire per project. You talk straight to the person doing the work. Skill and process range widely, so the hiring test matters most here.

  • 2

    Hire an agency

    A team with strategists, writers and often designers. A strong pick when copy is one part of a bigger launch or rebrand. Expect a structured process with more reviews and more people involved.

  • 3

    Subscribe to unlimited copy

    One flat monthly fee and a queue of requests, worked one or two at a time. Built for steady volume like blog posts, social posts and emails. Strategy usually starts from your brief.

  • 4

    Hire a senior specialist

    An experienced conversion writer who sets scope up front, by the project or as a fixed number of projects a month. Research comes before the draft. Fewer pieces, more thinking per page.

What to compare before you buy

Public price lists go stale, and many quotes are not public. Use this checklist on the call. The only dollar figures on this page are Pagetear's, taken from the live homepage.

  1. Who writes the work, and do they stay on the account?
  2. What counts as one request or one project: a page, an email sequence, an ad set, or a round of edits?
  3. How many requests can be active at once, and do revisions hold that slot?
  4. Is strategy included, or do you arrive with the message already decided?
  5. What is included in the monthly fee, and what is quoted separately?

Freelance rates swing too widely to publish a fair range. Agencies tend to quote after a scoping call. Three things move a quote most: how much research comes before the writing, how senior the writer is, and how many pieces you need each month.

A page that needs three rounds of rewrites from your own team costs more than the invoice. Count your hours too.

How to judge a copywriter

Before the samples, you can run a separate ten-minute check on whether the homepage explains the product: seven questions, no score.

Read samples for thinking

A clean sample shows the writer can write. It does not show they can think. Pick two samples and ask about each one.

  1. What was the brief, and where did you push back on it?
  2. Who was the buyer, and how did you learn what they cared about?
  3. Why this headline over the others you wrote?
  4. What would you change now?

Results are nice to hear but hard to check. The reasoning is what you can judge on a call.

Pay for a short test

Pick one small, real piece. A hero section or a single email works well. Pay the writer their normal rate and give every finalist the same brief.

Then judge three things. The questions they asked before writing. The reasoning they sent with the draft. How much you would change before it ships.

Some senior writers do not take tests. In that case, ask them to walk through your current page live, or to show a past brief and draft side by side.

Write a brief worth answering

A good writer will ask for most of this anyway. Have it ready and the first draft lands much closer.

Red flags to watch

These apply to every option on this page.

  • They quote a price before asking about your buyer.
  • They promise a lift in conversions before seeing your data.
  • Samples come with no brief, no context and no reasoning.
  • You cannot find out who will write your copy.
  • Scope is vague, so you cannot tell what one project includes.
  • They start writing before reading your sales notes, reviews or current page.
  • They cannot explain a headline choice in plain words.
  • Every sample sounds the same, whatever the product.

Questions for the first call

01Who will write my copy, and is it the same person each time?

A name, not a role. If it is a team, ask who edits and who signs off.

02What do you need from us before you start?

Listen for buyer calls, sales notes and your current numbers. A writer who needs nothing is guessing.

03How do you research a new SaaS product?

A clear order of steps. Customer calls, reviews, competitor pages and your own data.

04Can you walk me through one sample from brief to launch?

Specific choices and trade-offs, told in plain words.

05What counts as one project, and how do revisions work?

A clear unit of work and a revision process you could repeat back to your team.

06How long does a first draft take, and what slows it down?

A number of days, plus an honest answer about delays. Slow feedback is the usual one.

07When would you tell me you are not the right fit?

A real answer. Good writers know what they do not do well.

Hiring questions answered

How much does a SaaS copywriter cost?

Ask who writes, what one project includes, how many requests can be active, and whether revisions hold the slot. Pagetear's own plan, from the live homepage, is $2,000 a month for 1 project a week, $3,000 for 2, $4,000 for 3 and $5,000 for 4.

Should I hire a freelancer or an agency?

Hire a freelancer for a defined project you can brief well. Hire an agency when copy is part of a bigger launch with design and brand. If the message itself needs work, look at a senior specialist.

Is an unlimited copywriting subscription worth it?

Yes, if you need steady volume and already know what to say. Unlimited describes the queue. Most plans work one or two requests at a time, so output depends on how fast you give feedback.

Can AI write my SaaS copy?

AI writes a fast first draft. It does not know which claim your buyers believe or what your sales team hears every week. Use it for drafts and research, and hire a person for the judgment.

What should a copywriting brief include?

Who the buyer is, what they use today, the one action you want, your proof, common objections, customer words, pages you admire and your deadline.

How long does SaaS copy take?

It ranges from about two business days per request on a subscription to weeks per project at an agency. Research is the part not to rush.

Should I pay for a test project?

Yes. Pay the normal rate for a small, real piece. A paid test gets the same research and care as real work, so it tells you more.
